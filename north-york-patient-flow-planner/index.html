How the model works

  1. Patients arrive through two streams: booked appointments follow the selected morning-weighted or even pattern, and walk-ins are drawn from an early-day-weighted arrival profile.
  2. Every patient passes through one receptionist for intake, then joins a first-come, first-served queue for one of the procedure staff.
  3. Procedure times vary around each site's configured mean. The simulation repeats across multiple random days and reports averages and the 90th percentile.
  4. The seat calculation estimates people physically waiting, excluding the person at intake and people actively undergoing a procedure. People are not assumed to abandon the queue when all seats are occupied.
  5. In the coordination scenario, a configurable share of Sheppard walk-ins is offered the option of attending Finch before travelling. Added travel time is counted in the patient's time-to-procedure estimate.

This is a discrete-event planning approximation, not a validated operational digital twin. The assumed arrival pattern, service-time distribution, queue discipline and absence of clinical priority classes should be checked against local observations.

Design principles to test with patients and staff

Reduce uncertainty

Give a realistic wait range, explain the next step, and update it when the queue changes. Avoid false precision or an estimate that is never refreshed.

Make the process feel fair

Explain how booked appointments and walk-ins are handled, including legitimate clinical exceptions. Consistent explanations can prevent queue order from feeling arbitrary.

Make time less idle

Offer voluntary, low-effort activities and useful preparation information. These may improve the experience but do not replace staffing or flow improvements.

Reduce crowding and protect dignity

Track demand beyond the five-seat capacity at Sheppard. Maintain access for people who need seating, minimize sensitive conversations at reception, and consider sensory load.

Measure the real experience. Run brief, voluntary surveys about perceived wait, clarity of information, fairness, privacy, and overall experience. Compare responses with timestamp-derived actual waits, without collecting more personal or health information than necessary.

When to offer the other location

Test whether offering some Sheppard walk-ins a choice of Finch could reduce pressure across the pair without simply shifting the queue.

Operating principle: any alternative-site offer should be made before the patient travels, with informed choice and confirmation that the other site can perform the required collection. This model has no live capacity feed and must not be used to send patients between sites in real time.

Decision rule: offer a choice, do not direct

As an initial planning rule, consider offering Finch only when its estimated time to procedure including travel is at least 10 minutes shorter than staying at Sheppard, Finch has room to absorb more patients, and the required service is available. The patient must be free to decline without penalty.

Validate before recommending: compare the current wait and travel time, confirm Finch has capacity and offers the required service, and make sure the estimate is recent. This prototype has no live capacity connection.

Psychology matters: give patients a clear comparison of wait and travel time, explain why the alternative is being offered, and avoid creating a sense that they are losing their place or being pushed elsewhere. Do not offer a transfer to anyone already waiting at a site.

Coordination options beyond rerouting

Publish a consistent wait estimate

Present wait ranges for both locations only when the estimates are current. State the timestamp of the last update and avoid implying a guaranteed wait.

Use appointment mix to smooth demand

Compare demand by 15-minute interval, rather than relying on daily totals. Test slot spacing and protected walk-in capacity before changing booking rules.

Define a capacity-sharing protocol

Set explicit thresholds, service eligibility, patient consent, accessibility checks, and staff ownership before introducing cross-site recommendations.

Review outcomes together

Monitor median and 90th percentile wait, queue overflow, patients who leave, staff workload, and patient-reported clarity at both sites—not just the site receiving fewer arrivals.
